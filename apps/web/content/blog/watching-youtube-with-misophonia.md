I have misophonia, and my hardest trigger is mouth sounds. Not loud ones: the small, wet ones. A lip smack before a sentence. Someone talking while they eat. And the place I run into them most isn't the dinner table. It's YouTube.

## What a trigger feels like

People who don't have misophonia often imagine it as being a bit annoyed by noise. Researchers describe it differently: the 2022 consensus definition calls it "a disorder of decreased tolerance to specific sounds or stimuli associated with such sounds" ([Swedo et al., 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2022.841816/full)), and the reactions it describes are strong, immediate and hard to control.

## Why I built CalmEar

[Ange: what made you decide to build something, and roughly when. Keep it personal: the moment you thought "there must be a better way".]

CalmEar is a Chrome extension that detects mouth smacks and lip smacking in YouTube videos and reduces them as the video plays, while the voice stays clear. It runs on your computer, and the audio is never uploaded.

## What it changed, and what it doesn't fix

It isn't perfect, and I'd rather say so. It works on YouTube, in Chrome on a computer. It reduces mouth sounds rather than erasing every one, and some still get through, especially when they overlap with loud speech or music. [Ange: anything else you want people to know about its limits.]

## If this sounds familiar

If you recognised yourself in this, you're far from alone: in a UK study, about 18% of adults had misophonia symptoms that caused a significant burden in their life, and most had never heard the word ([Vitoratou et al., 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0282777)).
